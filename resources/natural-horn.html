The horn with no buttons

Before horns had valves, a horn was one very long brass tube wrapped into a circle.

No keys. No buttons. Players made every note with their lips, their ears, and one clever hand. This is the natural horn. Let's find out how it worked, and why its notes follow a secret pattern called the overtone series.

How it happened

From the forest to the orchestra

  1. 1600s

    Horns for hunting

    In France, hunters rode through the woods blowing big round horns to signal each other. The tube was coiled into a hoop so a rider could wear it over one shoulder.

  2. Early 1700s

    Into the orchestra

    Composers loved that bold outdoor sound and started adding horns to orchestras. There was one big problem: each horn could only play the notes of one key.

  3. 1700s

    Crooks to the rescue

    Instrument makers invented crooks: extra loops of tubing you could slide in and out. A longer crook makes a lower key. A shorter crook makes a higher key. Horn players carried a whole bag of them!

  4. About 1750

    The hand trick

    A horn player in Dresden named Anton Hampel figured out that moving your hand inside the bell could bend notes and fill in the gaps. This became known as hand horn playing.

  5. 1780s

    Mozart's horn concertos

    Mozart wrote four horn concertos for his friend Joseph Leutgeb, who played a natural horn with crooks and his hand in the bell. Mozart even scribbled jokes to him in the music.

  6. 1814–1818

    Valves arrive

    Heinrich Stölzel and Friedrich Blühmel invented valves. Pressing a valve instantly adds extra tubing, like swapping a crook in a split second. Over the next hundred years, valve horns took over.

  7. Today

    Still played!

    Natural horns are still played by musicians who want Mozart and Beethoven to sound the way those composers first heard it.

The big idea

Why can a tube only play certain notes?

Imagine you and a friend shaking a jump rope. You can make it swing in one big hump. Shake faster and it makes two humps, then three, then four. You can't make two-and-a-half humps!

The air inside a horn works the same way. When you buzz your lips faster, the air wiggles in more "humps," and you get a higher note. Each step up is called an overtone (or harmonic). The list of them, in order, is the overtone series.

Watch closely: more humps wiggle faster. Faster wiggles make higher notes. Each button plays that overtone on the crook you picked: the fundamental, then up an octave, a fifth, another octave, and a major third. Wiggle them all and you'll hear a major chord.

The overtone ladder

Here are the first 16 overtones of your horn. The rungs are spaced by how high they sound. Tap any rung to hear it.

Using the F crook. Change crooks in the crook swapper and the whole ladder slides up or down. The pattern of gaps never changes.

In tuneWobbly: between piano notes

Far apart down low

The bottom rungs are spread out, so you can only play bugle-call tunes down there.

Close together up high

From rung 8 to rung 16 the notes almost make a full scale. That's why old horn melodies sit way up high!

Rungs 7, 11, 13 and 14 don't line up with any piano key, so natural horn players fixed them with their hand.

The secret weapon

A hand in the bell

Pushing your hand further into the bell makes a note sound lower and a bit muffled. Hand horn players used this to reach the notes missing from the ladder and to fix the wobbly ones.

Listen to rung 8, then the same rung with the hand closing in. Horn players today still keep their right hand in the bell because of this tradition.

Hear the hand at work

Mozart wrote this tune for his friend Leutgeb, who played it on the E♭ crook. Here it is the way horn players read it today, written for horn in F. Most of the notes are open overtones, straight off the ladder. The notes circled in gold aren't on the ladder at all, so the player reaches them with the hand in the bell.
