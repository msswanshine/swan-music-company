Whole steps and half steps

Every scale follows a recipe of whole and half steps. Major goes whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half. Minor goes whole, half, whole, whole, half, whole, whole. A whole step spans two piano keys and a half step spans one.

The minor scale here is Aeolian, also called natural minor. It uses the same notes as its relative major, starting on the major scale’s sixth note, which is why one key signature fits both.

W is a whole step (2 semitones). H is a half step (1 semitone). The small numbers count semitones above the tonic.

How far each note bends

Equal temperament, the tuning a piano uses, spaces all twelve semitones identically. Resonant tuning, called just intonation in theory books, sets each note so its overtones line up with the tonic’s and reinforce each other. Most scale notes then land slightly above or below the equal spots. The unit is the cent, and 100 cents make one piano semitone. Bend amounts depend on major or minor and stay the same in every key, so changing the key signature only renames the notes and changes the pitch you hear.

Bars left of center mean lower the note from the equal-tempered pitch. Bars right of center mean raise it. Each gauge runs from 20 cents flat to 20 cents sharp.

    Using the numbers

    Major leans flat, minor leans sharp

    In major, the third (13.7 cents), sixth (15.6) and seventh (11.7) sit flat of equal temperament. In minor, the third (15.6), sixth (13.7) and seventh (17.6) sit sharp. The fourth and fifth move only 2 cents in either scale.

    The horn’s harmonic series shows it

    On the horn, the 5th partial sits a major third above the 4th partial in the ratio 5 to 4. The instrument itself resonates in that ratio, so the partial lands about 14 cents flat of equal temperament, the same amount as the major third in the table above.

    Match the room

    A piano cannot bend, so stay close to equal temperament on long notes when you play with one. Alone over a drone, or with other winds and strings, lean toward the resonant values on sustained thirds and sixths.

    Resonant values use five-limit just intonation measured from the tonic and compare against twelve-tone equal temperament. For the minor seventh the page uses 9/5, which matches how the major seventh is built. Some musicians prefer 16/9, which sits about 3.9 cents flat. Real ensembles keep adjusting as the harmony moves, so treat these numbers as a map and trust your ears for the final call.
